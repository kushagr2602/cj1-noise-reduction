Stage 01 / time domain

Capture the mixture

A microphone cannot tell voice from noise. It records their sum, one pressure value at a time, 16,000 times a second. The system only ever receives the bottom trace. The top two traces exist here so you can see what it has to separate.

Lower the SNR slider and the bottom trace stops looking like the voice at all. That is the situation the model trains on.

y(t) = s(t) + n(t)
y is the noisy recording, s the clean speech, n the noise. The goal is to recover s from y alone.

Stage 02 / frequency domain

Look at the whole clip's spectrum

The Fourier transform rewrites the clip as a sum of pure tones. Because it is linear, the voice and the noise stay separate in the sum, which is what makes filtering possible.

The voice shows as a comb of harmonics, spaced by the pitch (about 120 Hz), with the strongest peaks near the vowel formants. Broadband noise lifts the floor everywhere. Hum adds sharp spikes. The shaded band is the 300 to 3400 Hz range where most speech energy lives.

Y(ω) = S(ω) + N(ω)
One transform of the entire clip. It says which frequencies are present, but not when.

Stage 03 / time and frequency together

Slide a window along the clip

Speech changes every few tens of milliseconds, so one transform of the whole clip hides too much. The STFT cuts the clip into short overlapping frames, fades each frame in and out with a Hann window, and takes a Fourier transform of each one.

Drag the slider or press Scan. The amber trace is the frame being analysed. Its spectrum appears in the middle plot, and its column lights up in the spectrogram below. Try the 256 and 1024 window sizes in the top bar to see the time versus frequency trade-off.

X(ω,τ) = Σ x[n] · w[n−τ] · e^(−jωn)
w is the Hann window, τ is the frame position. Hop between frames is a quarter window, so each sample is covered by four frames.

Stage 04 / complex to real

Split magnitude from phase

Each STFT cell is a complex number. Its length is how loud that frequency is in that frame. Its angle is where in its cycle the tone sits. Noise shows up clearly in the magnitude picture, so the network is trained on magnitude only.

The phase picture is coloured by angle and dimmed where there is no energy. It looks like static, which is why it is hard to predict. The system keeps it unchanged and reattaches it at the end.

X = |X| · e^(j∠X)
Network input: |X| (log scale, normalised). Stored for later: ∠X.
Stage 05 / the enhancement step

Decide which cells are speech

For every time-frequency cell, a gain between 0 and 1 says how much of that cell to keep. Cells dominated by voice get a gain near 1. Cells dominated by noise get a gain near 0. Multiplying the noisy magnitude by this mask removes the noise.

The classic Wiener filter computes this gain from the true speech and noise power. That is the mask shown here. It needs s and n, which a real recording does not give you. The denoising autoencoder learns to predict the same mask from the noisy magnitude alone, after training on thousands of clean and noisy pairs.

M = |S|² / (|S|² + |N|²)
|Ŝ| = M · |Y|
Oracle mask for this demo. In the real system the DAE outputs M̂ and it is applied the same way.
Stage 06 / back to sound

Rebuild the waveform

The cleaned magnitude is paired with the stored noisy phase. Each frame goes through an inverse Fourier transform, is windowed again, and the overlapping frames are added together. The result is a clean waveform you can play.

Compare the green trace with the clean cyan one. At low SNR the leftover error comes mostly from the reused noisy phase, which is the main limit the proposal flags under audio reconstruction quality.

ŝ[n] = OLA( IFFT( |Ŝ| · e^(j∠Y) ) )
OLA is overlap-add, divided by the summed squared window so the level stays exact.
